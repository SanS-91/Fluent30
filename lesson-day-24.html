Interview — Why smart teams still make poor decisions

Host:People often assume that better decisions come from having smarter people in the room. Is that actually the main factor?

Guest:It helps, obviously, but I think we overestimate individual intelligence and underestimate process. A team can be full of capable people and still make a weak decision if the process rewards confidence more than challenge. The person who sounds most certain can become the center of gravity, even when the evidence is mixed.

Host:So confidence is being mistaken for quality?

Guest:Sometimes. The deeper problem is that confidence is visible, while uncertainty is harder to present well. If someone says, “I’m eighty percent sure,” we often hear the eighty and ignore the twenty. A strong process makes room for what would have to be true for the decision to fail.

Host:What does that look like in practice?

Guest:One useful habit is to actively look for weak signals. By that I mean small pieces of information that do not fit the main story yet. Maybe customer complaints are slightly different from what the dashboard suggests. Maybe one regional team is seeing a pattern before the central team sees it.

Host:But organizations have a lot of noise. You cannot escalate every small concern.

Guest:Exactly, and that is the tension. The answer is not to treat every signal as a crisis. The answer is to make it cheap to surface the signal before deciding whether it matters. Teams get into trouble when raising a concern feels socially expensive. People start filtering information before leadership has even seen it.

Host:That sounds like it could slow decisions down.

Guest:It can, if you apply the same process to everything. I think a better distinction is reversibility. If a decision is easy to reverse, speed is often more valuable than perfect analysis. Run the test, learn, adjust. But if the decision is expensive or hard to unwind, you want more challenge before commitment.

Host:So not every decision deserves the same amount of debate.

Guest:Right. Treating all decisions as equally important creates two problems. Either the organization becomes painfully slow, or people stop respecting the process because it feels bureaucratic. The level of scrutiny should match the cost of being wrong and the cost of reversing course.

Host:What about after the decision? How should teams learn from the result?

Guest:This is where outcome bias becomes dangerous. A bad process can produce a good result because you got lucky. A good process can produce a bad result because uncertainty was real. If you judge only by the outcome, you reward luck and punish disciplined thinking.

Host:Then what should a review look at?

Guest:Look at what information was available at the time, which assumptions mattered, what alternatives were considered, and what would have changed the decision. A simple decision journal helps because it preserves the reasoning before hindsight rewrites the story.

Host:If you had to reduce all of this to one principle?

Guest:Separate the quality of the process from the quality of the outcome. Then design the process around the kind of decision you are actually making.

“center of gravity” The person or idea that begins to dominate the discussion.
“what would have to be true...” Tests the assumptions required for a conclusion to hold.
“make it cheap to surface...” Reduce the social or procedural cost of raising a concern.
“hard to unwind” Difficult or expensive to reverse after commitment.
“outcome bias” Judging a past decision mainly by what happened afterward.
“hindsight rewrites the story” After knowing the result, people remember the earlier uncertainty less accurately.
