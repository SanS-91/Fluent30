Day 04 · Linking & Reductions

Hear the sentence
as one stream.

Native speech does not arrive as neatly separated words. Sounds link across word boundaries, consonants change before neighboring sounds, and frequent phrases compress into faster forms. Today you train your ear to follow those transformations without losing meaning.

Step 6 · Decode

Transcript locked

Complete at least two full listens before revealing the transcript.

Transcript — Sam & Priya

Sam: Did you want to grab something to eat now, or are you gonna stay and finish the report?

Priya: I was gonna finish it first, but I think I've got another hour at least.

Sam: Then why don't we just go now? We've gotta eat at some point.

Priya: True. I just have to send one email before we leave.

Sam: That's fine. I'll pick up my stuff and meet you downstairs.

Priya: Actually, can we make it fifteen minutes? I need to call Ben back too.

Sam: Sure. Do you wanna go to that Thai place we tried last month?

Priya: Yeah, that works. I didn't get a chance to eat there properly last time.

Sam: Perfect. I'll book a table while you're wrapping up.

“Did you want to…” → “Didja wanna…” Assimilation plus reduction can transform a perfectly familiar phrase into a very different sound.
“going to” → “gonna” A common conversational reduction. Recognize it instantly as a chunk.
“we've got to” → “we've gotta” The phrase compresses because “got to” is highly predictable in context.
“have to” → “hafta” The /v/ changes before /t/, producing a faster /f/ sound.
“pick up” → “pick-up” The final consonant of “pick” flows directly into the vowel of “up”.
“didn't get” → “didn' get” /t/ may disappear before another consonant in fast speech.
