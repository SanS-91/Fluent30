Step 2 · Debate Toolkit

Identify the move, not just the sentence.

10 min
Claim
A common rule reduces coordination friction.
The position the speaker wants you to accept.
Support
New hires need predictable access to colleagues.
A reason, example or mechanism supporting the claim.
Rebuttal
Presence does not guarantee useful collaboration.
Directly attacks the logic or evidence behind the other side.
Concession
I agree that shared time matters.
Accepts a valid part of the opposing case.
Counter-rebuttal
But without a baseline, overlap becomes fragile.
Responds to the rebuttal and restores the original case.
Steelman
The strongest version of your argument is...
Represents the opposing position fairly before responding.

Transcript — One hybrid rule or team-level autonomy?

Maya:I think the company needs a clear baseline — for example, three shared office days. The point is not that people cannot work effectively at home. The point is that coordination becomes much harder when everyone is making independent choices about when to be present.

Daniel:I agree that coordination matters, but I think a company-wide rule solves the wrong problem. Different teams do different work. A design team running workshops may benefit from more in-person time. A data team doing long blocks of focused work may not. Why force the same pattern on both?

Maya:Because the organization is larger than the team. People collaborate across functions. New hires need access to colleagues they do not yet know. If every team optimizes only for itself, the company can end up with a coordination tax that nobody owns.

Daniel:That is fair, but being in the same building does not automatically create useful interaction. We have all had office days filled with video calls. I would rather define the collaboration we need — shared workshops, mentoring time, cross-team overlap — and then design attendance around that.

Maya:My concern is that once attendance becomes entirely optional or team-specific, those shared moments become fragile. The people with the most flexibility may show up, while others do not. A common rule creates predictability. You know when you can find people.

Daniel:But predictability can be created with anchor time without requiring the same three days for everyone. A global team might need overlap with another time zone more than physical presence. And for some employees, a long commute creates a real cost that should be justified by the value of being there.

Maya:I do not disagree with that. The strongest version of your argument is that attendance should be tied to the work, not to symbolism. I agree. What I am pushing back on is the idea that teams can coordinate perfectly without any company-level constraint.

Daniel:And the strongest version of yours is that shared norms reduce friction across teams, especially for people who are new or less connected. I agree with that too. My concern is when the norm becomes a proxy for performance — when being seen in the office starts to count more than actual output.

Maya:That is a management problem, though, not necessarily an argument against shared days.

Daniel:True, but policy changes behavior. If leaders mandate presence, employees will reasonably assume presence matters. So the policy has to be explicit about what the office is for.

Maya:Then maybe the useful middle ground is a minimum amount of predictable overlap, with teams deciding the rest based on the work.

Daniel:I could support that, provided the overlap is designed around real collaboration rather than simply counting attendance.

“solves the wrong problem” The response challenges whether the proposed solution addresses the real cause.
“the organization is larger than the team” Local optimization may create cross-team costs.
“coordination tax” Extra friction, time or effort created by poor coordination.
“the strongest version of your argument” Signals a steelman before responding.
“proxy for performance” An indirect signal being treated as if it were actual performance.
“provided...” Introduces a condition for agreement.
