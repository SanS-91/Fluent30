Transcript — When a good metric becomes a bad target

Metrics are useful because they compress reality. A manager cannot watch every customer interaction, inspect every process, or personally evaluate every decision, so organizations use indicators: response time, conversion rate, utilization, defects, margin, retention, output per employee.

The problem begins when an indicator stops being treated as evidence and starts being treated as the objective itself. Once people know which number determines attention, rewards or promotion, they naturally adapt their behavior around it.

Sometimes that is exactly what management wants. If a team has been slow to respond to customers, a response-time target can create useful urgency. But the target may also produce behavior that improves the number while weakening the service: closing tickets too early, giving shallow answers, or avoiding complicated cases.

Notice the distinction. The metric is not necessarily wrong. The incentive around the metric changes how informative it is. Before the target, response time may tell you something about service quality. After the target becomes highly visible, response time also tells you how strongly people are managing the metric.

This becomes more serious when the metric is narrow and the underlying objective is broad. Sales volume is easier to count than customer lifetime value. Utilization is easier to count than whether people are working on the right problem. Number of projects completed is easier to count than whether those projects created durable value.

There is also a timing issue. A measure can improve quickly while the cost appears later. Heavy discounting may lift quarterly volume but weaken future pricing. Cutting training may improve this year's cost base while making next year's execution less reliable. Pushing people to maximize utilization can raise short-term output while leaving no capacity for learning, maintenance or unexpected work.

None of this means organizations should stop measuring performance. The practical lesson is almost the opposite: use multiple signals, separate diagnostic metrics from hard targets, and ask what behavior a target will encourage before attaching consequences to it.

A strong metric system does not merely tell people what to maximize. It helps decision-makers notice when improving one number is quietly damaging the outcome the number was supposed to represent.

“compress reality” Reduce a complex situation into a small number of indicators.
“treated as evidence” Used as one signal, not assumed to be the objective itself.
“manage the metric” Change behavior mainly to improve the measured number.
“narrow metric / broad objective” The measurement captures only one slice of a much larger goal.
“the cost appears later” Short-term improvement may create delayed negative effects.
“attach consequences” Link the metric to rewards, penalties or decisions.
