Transcript — The paradox of convenience

We usually assume that convenience improves quality of life. If something saves us time, reduces effort or removes friction, the logic seems straightforward: life should become easier.

And to a large extent, that's true. Online banking, navigation apps and same-day delivery remove tasks that once required far more time and energy. It would be hard to argue that none of this has improved daily life.

But there is a more complicated effect that is easy to miss. As certain tasks become easier, our expectations often rise with them. If sending a message takes seconds, we start to expect replies in minutes. If delivery becomes faster, waiting two days can suddenly feel slow.

In other words, convenience doesn't simply remove friction. It can also redefine what counts as acceptable friction. The standard moves.

This may help explain why people can have access to more time-saving tools and still feel constantly rushed. The tools reduce the time required for individual tasks, but the number of tasks — and the expectation that we complete them quickly — may increase at the same time.

Of course, that doesn't mean convenience is bad. The stronger point is that its benefits are partly shaped by the expectations that grow around it. Saving time only improves quality of life if some of that time remains available to us.

So the question may not be “How can we make everything faster?” It may be “Which forms of friction are actually worth removing, and which ones protect our attention, patience or boundaries?”

“to a large extent” Mostly true, but not completely or without exceptions.
“the standard moves” Expectations change, so what once felt fast may later feel slow.
“may help explain why…” A cautious causal interpretation, not a proven explanation.
“the stronger point is…” Signals the speaker's refined central argument.
“partly shaped by…” One factor influences the outcome, but does not fully determine it.
“protect our attention” Some friction may create useful limits against constant demands.
