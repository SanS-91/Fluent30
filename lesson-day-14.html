Transcript — Customer retention presentation

What I'd like to do today is look at why customer retention has softened over the last two quarters and what we can do about it.

Let me start with the data. Overall retention is still healthy at eighty-seven percent, but that headline number hides a fairly clear problem. Among customers who joined in the last six months, retention has fallen from eighty-four to seventy-six percent.

The key point here is that the decline isn't spread evenly across the customer base. It's concentrated in newer customers, particularly those who completed onboarding without speaking to a support adviser.

Now, if we look at the feedback, the pattern becomes even clearer. Customers are not mainly complaining about price. They're telling us the first few weeks feel confusing: too many emails, unclear next steps, and not enough human contact.

What this means is that we probably don't have an acquisition problem yet. We have an early-experience problem. If we increase acquisition spend before fixing that, we'll simply bring more customers into a journey that is already underperforming.

So my recommendation is fairly simple. First, redesign the first thirty days around three clear milestones. Second, add one proactive adviser call in week two. And third, test the new journey with one customer segment before scaling it.

I don't expect this to solve everything immediately, but if the pilot improves six-month retention by even four or five points, the economics are significantly better than spending the same amount on additional acquisition.

To sum up, retention is still solid overall, but the trend among newer customers is moving in the wrong direction. The priority should be to fix the early customer experience first, measure the impact, and only then decide whether to increase acquisition spend.

“that headline number hides…” The overall metric looks fine, but a sub-segment contains a meaningful problem.
“the pattern becomes even clearer” The speaker is adding another evidence source that strengthens the argument.
“What this means is…” The presentation moves from evidence to interpretation.
“underperforming journey” The customer experience is producing weaker results than expected.
“before scaling it” Test with a smaller group before applying it broadly.
“moving in the wrong direction” The trend is deteriorating even if the current absolute level is still acceptable.
