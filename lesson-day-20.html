Test A — Product launch review

Okay, before we sign off, I want to check the launch decision one more time. The product itself is ready, but the support team is asking for another week because the new help-center content isn't complete.

My concern is that if we move the launch, we lose the campaign slot we already paid for. On the other hand, launching with weak support content could create a poor first experience.

The compromise I'm proposing is this: keep the public launch on Thursday, but limit the first campaign to existing customers and partners. That gives Support four extra days before we open the wider campaign next Monday.

Marketing can live with that, provided the audience is still at least twelve thousand contacts. Support says they can finish the priority articles by Friday afternoon, and the remaining lower-priority content can follow next week.

So Thursday stays. Marketing confirms the reduced audience by three today. Support publishes the priority articles by five Friday. I’ll send the final launch note once both are confirmed.

Test B — Interview: automation and work

When people talk about automation, they often jump straight to the question of whether jobs disappear. I think that's understandable, but it's too narrow.

In most organizations, jobs are bundles of tasks. Some tasks are repetitive and easy to automate. Others depend on judgment, context, trust or coordination. So the more useful question is not “Will this job disappear?” but “Which parts of this job change?”

We've seen this before with technology. Spreadsheets didn't eliminate finance teams. They changed what finance teams spent time doing. Email didn't eliminate management. It changed the speed and volume of communication.

Now, that doesn't mean the transition is painless. Some roles will shrink, some skills will become less valuable, and people may need to move into different kinds of work. But it's still too early to treat every automation gain as a direct one-for-one reduction in headcount.

The organizations that handle this well will probably be the ones that redesign work deliberately: automate low-value repetition, preserve human judgment where it matters, and help people move toward the parts of the job that create more value.
