Day 03 · Weak Forms

The words are there.
They just got smaller.

In natural English, grammar words such as to, for, and, can, have, you are often unstressed. Their vowels weaken, their consonants link, and the sentence rhythm is carried by the important words. Today you train yourself to hear those “small” words without waiting for their dictionary pronunciation.

Step 2 · Weak Forms

Meet the sounds that disappear.

15 min

English rhythm highlights content words. Function words often reduce toward the neutral vowel /ə/ (schwa). Do not expect the full dictionary form unless the word is being emphasized.

to
/tə/

“need to leave” → “need-tə-leave”

for
/fər/

“works for me” → “works-fər-me”

and
/ən/

“coffee and lunch” → “coffee-n-lunch”

can
/kən/

“I can make it” → “I-kən-make-it”

have
/əv/

“should have checked” → “should-əv-checked”

you
/jə/

“do you want…” → “d'ya-want…”

Word Weak form Strong form appears when…
can /kən/ contrast/emphasis: “Yes, I CAN.”
to /tə/ contrast/emphasis: “I said TO, not from.”
for /fər/ contrast/emphasis: “It's FOR Nina.”

Transcript — Jordan's voice message

Hey Nina, just wanted to check what works for you tomorrow. I was supposed to meet Daniel at nine, but he asked if we can push it back to ten.

That actually works better for me because I've got a couple of things to finish before I leave the house. If you're free, do you want to meet at the café around eleven-thirty?

We can go over the notes for Friday and then grab lunch somewhere nearby. I don't have to be back at the office until two, so there's no rush.

Oh, and I meant to ask you about the client file. You don't have to send the whole thing — just the summary and the latest numbers should be enough.

Anyway, let me know what works for you. If eleven-thirty is too early, we can make it twelve.

“works for you” → “works fər yə” Both for and you weaken because the stress lands on “works”.
“supposed to” → “supposed tə” The vowel in to reduces to schwa /ə/.
“if we can” → “if we kən” can is weak unless the speaker emphasizes ability or contrast.
“and then” → “ən then” and is often reduced to /ən/ or even just /n/.
“have to” → “hafta” In fast speech, “have to” commonly compresses heavily.
“let me” → “lemme” Frequent chunks become highly compressed because listeners recognize them as a unit.
